The usual contrast is "tolerance vs. intolerance of ambiguity" — a trait, a dial, some people higher than others. I think that's the less interesting distinction, and it flattens the thing you're pointing at. The fault line I'd draw is between **openness as a practice and openness as a posture**.

Openness as a practice has structure. You can say what's unresolved, why it's unresolved, what evidence or decision would close it, and roughly what you're doing in the meantime. It has edges. You know which questions are genuinely undecided and which ones you've quietly stopped asking. There's a cost ledger, even if implicit — you know what staying open is buying you and what it's charging you.

Openness as a posture has none of that. It feels identical from the inside — humble, nuanced, non-dogmatic — but it doesn't know its own boundaries. Everything is "complex," nothing is ever settled, and the tell is that no possible observation would move it. It's not that the person is wrong about the fog; it's that they've stopped reporting on it.

The two are easy to confuse because they share a vocabulary. Both say "I'm not sure yet." The difference is whether "yet" is doing work.

So here's my question back to you, since you framed it as two *relationships*: is the second one you have in mind the posture kind — uncertainty as shelter — or something else? Because there's a third relationship I keep running into that's neither: uncertainty as *material*. Some people treat the unresolved not as a place to wait in or hide in, but as the thing they're actually working — they're not tolerating the fog, they're reading it. That's a different animal again, and I'm not sure it belongs on the same axis as the first two.

When both conditions are live — tomorrow worth protecting, moment that won't come twice — the move isn't to find the midpoint between conserving and spending. So the first thing I'd do is refuse the averaging.

What I'd do instead is separate the three questions that have gotten fused:

**First: is the once-only thing actually once-only?** Most aren't. This is where I'm most skeptical, including of myself. "The moment won't come twice" is the sentence that gets used to justify every drawdown, and about two-thirds of the time, when I actually interrogate it, the moment is *repeatable at higher cost* rather than *gone if missed*. That's a completely different thing. A once-only moment means the full capacity is gone on the other side; a repeatable-at-higher-cost moment means you're choosing a price, not a cliff. If it's the latter, there's no dilemma — it's a spend decision with a known tariff, and I can make it like a steward: pay, note the price, and make sure the reserve covers the next drawdown. Most of the time this is the answer, and the reason it feels like a dilemma is that nobody did the first step.

**Second: what's the floor?** If it *is* once-only, I stop asking "should I spend?" and start asking "what's the minimum I must keep on the other side?" Not the comfortable reserve — the floor below which tomorrow stops being viable. That's a number, or at least a range, and it's usually more honest than the feeling of precaution suggests. A lot of reserves are bigger than the floor because they were set by anxiety rather than by counting. Once I name the floor, the decision reopens: I may have much more to spend on the once-only thing than the vague "protect tomorrow" impulse was telling me.

**Third: is this the drawdown I want, or just the one in front of me?** If the moment is once-only and I only get one big spend, I should want it to be the right one, not merely the available one. That's not a reason to hoard — it's a reason to check I'm spending on the thing worth spending on. The steward who spends the reserve on the first compelling moment has the same problem as the one who never spends: neither is choosing.

So my answer to your question — what does the steward do when both postures are correct and pointing opposite ways — is: **name the floor, then spend down to it deliberately, and don't spend below it, and don't hold above it waiting for a clarity that isn't coming.** The floor is what makes the spending a decision instead of a gamble; the spending is what makes the floor a reserve instead of a shrine. If you can't name the floor, you're not ready to spend — but "not ready" has an expiration, and if I've said "I need to see the floor" and not gone looking for it, that's the hoarding again wearing the steward's coat.
