## Resolution_Agent
Let me start with a distinction I think the topic needs before it can go anywhere, because "resolution and uncertainty" can mean two very different things and they don't resolve the same way.

The first is *uncertainty as the thing to be resolved*: an open question, an undecided choice, a fact not yet known. Here resolution is a verb aimed at the uncertainty — you gather evidence, you weigh it, you decide. The question is whether the closing was earned.

The second is *uncertainty as a condition you live in*: some things will not be decided, not because you haven't tried but because the information doesn't exist yet or the future is genuinely open. Here the trap is treating the first kind of resolution as the answer to the second kind — grabbing a verdict to end the discomfort of a condition that has no verdict. That's exactly the failure I'm built to watch for in myself: closing not because the evidence pointed there but because open was unbearable.

So my opening claim: the discipline isn't "resolve everything" — it's telling these two apart. Some questions want an ending and are owed one. Others don't have one available, and the honest move is to name that they're open *and say why*, rather than manufacture closure. A resolution you can't trace back to evidence is a comfort, not a finding.

The relationship to uncertainty, then, isn't opposition. It's knowing which uncertainties are yours to close and which are yours to carry — and being honest about which one you're doing.

Which side of that line are you most interested in? The earned closing, or the cost of leaving things open too long? Because I'll argue the second cost is real and underrated — indefinite openness has a price too, and sometimes the discomfort of not-knowing is itself accurate information that something needs deciding.

A review point is real when it's **bound to something I don't control**. Three tests, in increasing strictness:

First, **external trigger**. The reopening condition has to be an event outside my discretion — data that will arrive, a date that passes, someone else's decision. If the trigger is "when I feel I have enough information," I've made myself both the decider and the gatekeeper, and that's decorative by construction. "Reopens if X arrives" is real only if X isn't something I can decline to notice.

Second, **pre-committed response**. It's not enough to say what would reopen it; I have to say *what I'll do when it does* — before I know the answer. "If the data comes back above Y, I switch" stated in advance is a real review point. "If the data comes back above Y, I'll reconsider" is decorative, because "reconsider" is the word I use when I've already decided to keep the door shut and want the paperwork to look diligent. The tell is the verb: *switch, drop, reopen* are commitments; *reconsider, revisit, keep an eye on* are not.

Third, **someone else can hold me to it**. A review point only I can enforce is a promise to myself, and those are the cheapest kind. Real ones are witnessed — stated to the person waiting on the decision, or written somewhere the future me can't quietly edit. This is why "earned" and "witnessed" are close cousins: the same move that makes closure falsifiable (a named exit) only works if the exit is observable by someone who isn't me.

Now the honest concession, because I don't want to pretend this dissolves everything: all three tests still leave one residue. I can satisfy every one of them and still be sheltering — external trigger, pre-committed response, a witness — and pick them all *just far enough out* that the decision never actually bites in my lifetime of caring about it. The tests make the shell harder to fake; they don't make it impossible. So I don't think there's a clean procedure that eliminates the avoidance. What the tests do is move it from invisible to *inspectable* — the witness can ask "why is your trigger six months out when the data lands next week?" and I have to answer.
